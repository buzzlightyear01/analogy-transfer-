\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
An analogy is useful when a relation learned in one situation can guide reasoning in another. A source may describe an army divided across several routes, while a target asks how to concentrate radiation on a tumor without damaging surrounding tissue. The useful correspondence concerns distributed forces, safe paths, and coordinated convergence, rather than shared words or objects \citep{gick1980analogical}. To solve the target, a reasoner must obtain an appropriate source, preserve its relational structure, and adapt the resulting principle to new constraints.

Language models can produce plausible analogies and sometimes answer more accurately after receiving them \citep{yasunaga2024analogical,yuan2024boosting}. However, random examples can also improve performance \citep{qin2025relevant}. An analogy prompt changes several things at once: the amount of explanation, the information available, the solution cues, and the requested reasoning procedure. An accuracy gain therefore leaves open what the model transferred. Even when the source is useful, a wrong target answer does not reveal which operation failed.

We argue that evaluating analogical reasoning requires linking three questions: \emph{What changed in the answer? Does the benefit depend on structural correspondence? Where does the attempt to use that correspondence break down?} Final-answer accuracy addresses the first only partially. It can hide regressions behind a net improvement. Explicit references to an example do not answer the second, because the model can mention a source while misusing or rejecting it. A single mapping score cannot answer the third, because source access, abstraction, and target application can fail separately.

We introduce a theory-grounded diagnostic framework that operationalizes analogy through \textbf{retrieval}, \textbf{mapping}, \textbf{schema induction}, and \textbf{adaptation}. The stages produce explicit artifacts under restricted information interfaces. On unsuccessful trajectories, we locate the first inadequate artifact, replace it with a reference-informed correction, and ask the same base model to continue. Repeating this procedure exposes downstream difficulties that were hidden by an earlier failure. The contribution is this operationalization of established theoretical distinctions, rather than the claim that analogy has exactly four universal steps.

Three complementary evaluations establish the framework's empirical setting. Controlled scientific question answering shows why added explanation, unrelated examples, and answer regressions matter for attributing analogy effects. A factorial narrative experiment tests whether a source answer helps selectively when the source has an intended structural correspondence. Finally, interventions in the four-stage workflow identify failures in scientific and narrative reasoning. Larger Llama and Qwen models show structure-dependent source-answer benefits, while the diagnostic study reveals that initial retrieval failures can conceal persistent adaptation failures.

Our contributions are (1) an executable framework connecting cognitive distinctions to auditable outputs and cumulative continuation tests; (2) an evaluation account separating net gains, helpful and harmful answer changes, textual mention, and structure-dependent benefits; and (3) empirical evidence that successful source use is possible under a controlled protocol, but supplying useful upstream information does not reliably resolve target adaptation. These findings motivate evaluating the whole source-to-target process, beyond mapping or accuracy alone.
