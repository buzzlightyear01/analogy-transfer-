\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
A solved example can help a model answer a new question, introduce a misleading rule, or supply information that the model never uses. These possibilities matter for analogy-based assistance: its intended benefit comes from transferring a useful relation across situations, rather than merely adding text or copying an answer. Yet target accuracy alone does not distinguish them. In our scientific question-answering study, a 300-word teacher analogy changes 86 Mistral answers from incorrect to correct and 41 from correct to incorrect, relative to chain-of-thought (CoT), on the same 448 questions. The net gain hides substantial regressions, and a non-analogical explanation achieves slightly higher accuracy than the analogy.

Analogical prompting has shown that language models can benefit from self-generated exemplars \citep{yasunaga2024analogical}, while teacher analogies can support scientific concept understanding \citep{yuan2024boosting}. However, randomly selected examples can also help \citep{qin2025relevant}. A contrast against one baseline therefore leaves competing explanations: added information, a changed reasoning instruction, a source-answer cue, or transfer through the intended correspondence. Even when a source is appropriate, an unsuccessful answer leaves a further question: was the source poorly chosen, its relation lost, its schema inadequate, or its application incorrect?

We connect three evaluation questions: \emph{what changed in the answer, does the benefit depend on the designed correspondence, and what deficiencies occur along an explicit transfer attempt?} These require different evidence. Matched correctness transitions describe improvements and regressions. Controlled source assignment and answer availability test selective source benefits. Intermediate artifacts and continuation tests characterize an observable source-to-target path.

Our central contribution is a \textbf{theory-grounded diagnostic framework} operationalizing source construction, mapping, schema induction, and adaptation. It translates established cognitive distinctions into recorded outputs with explicit information contracts. Mapping compares solved sources; schema induction abstracts their common structure; adaptation reconnects that structure to the target. We inspect unsuccessful paths and replace inadequate artifacts with oracle-authored references before asking the same base model to continue. The resulting record separates the first assigned deficiency from the outcome and deficiencies of subsequent corrected continuations.

Three studies support this account. Scientific question-answering controls motivate careful attribution. A factorial narrative experiment provides positive evidence of selective source-answer benefit in several models. A diagnostic study then examines the imposed four-stage workflow. Its contribution is diagnostic resolution rather than an accuracy advantage: the direct and staged baselines are not compute-matched, and supplying a reference-informed artifact is external assistance. The studies use different cohorts and protocols; their relationship is conceptual rather than a causal comparison of supplied versus self-generated sources.

We contribute (1) an executable, artifact-based operationalization of theoretical distinctions in analogy; (2) an evaluation design separating outcome changes, structural selectivity, and stage-level continuation outcomes; and (3) empirical analyses with paired accounting, conditional uncertainty estimates, and explicit separation of natural performance from oracle assistance. The framework makes a designed process inspectable without claiming that language models internally reason in four discrete stages.
